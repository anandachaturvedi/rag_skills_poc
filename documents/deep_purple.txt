The Deep Purple

The town of Kettlewick sat in a shallow valley where three rivers used to
meet before the old dam changed their courses, and it was the kind of place
most travelers passed through without ever really stopping. Its one paved
road ran past a hardware store, a diner that only served breakfast, and a
church with a bell tower that hadn't rung in decades. Behind the church,
past a crooked iron gate nobody bothered locking anymore, was the town's old
communal orchard - dozens of untended apple and pear trees that had been
planted generations ago by families who no longer lived there.

Desmond had grown up two streets over from the orchard, and even now, well
into his thirties, he still walked its paths most evenings after work. It
was less about the fruit, which was usually wormy or half-eaten by birds,
and more about the quiet. The orchard was one of the few places in
Kettlewick where the noise of his own thoughts seemed to soften, where the
particular ache of living in a town everyone else had left behind didn't
follow him quite so closely.

That evening, the light was doing something unusual. Late autumn sun came in
low and amber through the branches, throwing long shadows across the grass,
and the air smelled like wet leaves and distant woodsmoke. Desmond walked
his usual loop, past the leaning pear tree that never bore fruit, past the
cluster of crabapples the local kids used for makeshift slingshot ammunition,
until he reached the far corner of the orchard where the oldest trees stood.

Nobody really remembered who had planted the trees in that corner. They grew
taller and wilder than the rest, their bark rougher, their branches tangled
together like they were holding onto each other for support. Desmond rarely
went that far in, mostly because the ground got uneven and the light didn't
reach as well. But something about the evening - maybe the color of the sky,
maybe just restlessness - pulled him past his usual turnaround point.

It was there, beneath a tree he didn't recognize, that he saw it. Hanging
low on a branch, almost at eye level, was a single apple, and it was not a
color he had ever seen on fruit before. It wasn't the deep red of the
orchard's Cortlands, or the pale yellow-green of the old Gravenstein trees
near the gate. It was purple - a rich, saturated purple, closer to the color
of a ripe plum or a twilight sky just after sunset, with faint darker
streaks running down toward the stem like veins.

Desmond stood still for a long moment, half convinced his eyes were playing
tricks on him in the strange evening light. He'd lived near this orchard his
entire life and had never once heard of a purple apple tree, wild graft or
otherwise. He glanced around, almost expecting to see a sign or a ribbon
tied to the branch, some explanation left by a horticulture student or an
eccentric neighbor. There was nothing. Just the tree, the fading light, and
the apple.

He reached up and closed his hand around it gently, testing its weight. It
felt ordinary enough - firm, slightly cool from the evening air, with the
faint waxy texture of any other apple close to ripeness. When he brought it
near his face, though, the smell surprised him. Instead of the usual sharp,
tart apple scent, it carried something warmer, almost like cinnamon mixed
with something he couldn't quite place - clove, maybe, or old paper.

Desmond didn't pick it. He turned it once more between his fingers, studying
the way the fading sunlight caught the deep color of its skin, and then let
it go, watching it settle back against the branch. Some instinct, half
superstition and half simple caution, told him it wasn't his to take. His
grandmother, before she passed, used to tell him stories about the orchard
having its own quiet rules, unspoken agreements between the town and
whatever had first taken root there. He'd always assumed those were just
stories meant to keep restless kids from stripping the trees bare. Standing
under that strange purple apple in the dying light, he wasn't so sure
anymore.

He stayed a while longer, until the amber light faded into the flatter grey
of dusk and the first few stars became visible through gaps in the canopy.
Somewhere behind him, toward the church, the wind picked up and rattled the
old iron gate against its post, a sound he'd heard a thousand times before
but that felt different tonight. Eventually, the cold convinced him to
leave. He took one last look at the tree - already harder to make out in the
dimming light - and made his way back along the familiar path toward the
gate, the diner, the quiet streets of Kettlewick waiting beyond.

He never told anyone about the apple, not at first. Part of him worried
they'd think he was imagining things, the way people sometimes did about a
town that had a way of turning ordinary evenings into something stranger if
you let it. But every so often, on evenings when the light came in low and
amber through the trees, Desmond found himself walking back to that same
corner of the orchard, just to see if the purple apple was still there,
still hanging quietly on its branch, waiting for someone who understood it
wasn't meant to be picked.
