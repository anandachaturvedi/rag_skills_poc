In the center of the crowded city of Aramore stood a small workshop that

almost nobody noticed. Its sign simply read "Elias Venn - Clocks Repaired."

The windows were narrow, the paint was peeling, and the doorbell had not

worked properly for years.

Elias had been repairing clocks since he was sixteen.

His workshop was filled with strange machines: brass pendulums, wooden

cuckoo clocks, pocket watches, tower-clock mechanisms, and hundreds of

tiny gears stored in labelled drawers. Customers often complained that the

shop was too cluttered, but Elias knew exactly where everything belonged.

Behind the workshop was a garden.

It was not an ordinary garden. Instead of flowers, Elias grew plants whose

leaves opened and closed according to the movement of nearby clocks. Some

responded to the ticking of watches. Others reacted to the slow swinging of

pendulums.

The garden had belonged to his grandmother, who had taught him that time was

not merely something measured by clocks.

"Every living thing keeps time," she used to say.

Elias had never completely understood what she meant.

One winter morning, he discovered a broken pocket watch inside a drawer he

had not opened in years. The watch had belonged to his grandmother. Its

hands had stopped at exactly 3:17.

He repaired the mechanism but noticed something unusual.

Whenever the watch began ticking, every plant in the garden turned toward

the workshop.

Elias carried the watch outside.

The plants slowly moved.

He tested the watch several times. Each time he stopped it, the plants

returned to their normal positions. Each time he restarted it, they leaned

toward him.

Curious, Elias began experimenting.

He placed different clocks around the garden. The plants responded

differently to each one. A grandfather clock made the vines climb upward.

A pocket watch caused tiny white flowers to open. A brass alarm clock made

the leaves fold tightly.

But his grandmother's pocket watch produced the strangest reaction.

At exactly 3:17 each afternoon, the entire garden became still.

No leaves moved.

No insects flew between the plants.

Even the wind seemed to disappear.

Then, after precisely one minute, everything returned to normal.

Elias began recording the event.

For thirty days, the same thing happened.

On the thirty-first day, however, something changed.

At 3:17, the garden did not become still.

Instead, every plant began moving at once.

The vines pulled away from the walls. Flowers opened. Branches bent toward

the center of the garden, revealing a stone platform Elias had never seen

before.

On the platform was a small metal box.

He opened it carefully.

Inside was a letter written by his grandmother.

She explained that the garden had been cultivated by their family for

generations. Each generation was supposed to discover the garden's secret

at the right moment. The plants, she wrote, were sensitive not to ordinary

time but to memories carried through mechanical clocks.

The pocket watch had belonged to Elias's grandfather before it belonged to

her.

The letter ended with a simple instruction.

"Do not try to stop the garden. Listen to it."

Elias sat quietly until evening.

Over the following months, he began studying the relationship between the

garden and the clocks in his workshop. He discovered that different

mechanisms produced different patterns of movement. By carefully observing

the plants, he could tell when a clock was losing time, when a spring was

about to break, and even when a particular gear had become misaligned.

Soon people began visiting the workshop from across the city.

They brought clocks that nobody else could repair.

Elias never told them about the garden.

He simply listened to the ticking, watched the plants, and made the

necessary adjustments.

Years later, when Elias finally retired, he taught his apprentice the same

lesson his grandmother had taught him.

"Every living thing keeps time."

This time, he understood exactly what she meant.
